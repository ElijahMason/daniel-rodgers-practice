A material is a decision.

Force, heat, wear, and the machine that will make the real part.

Daniel Rodgers picks a material for the question in front of him, and he picks again for the factory. The first physical version may use a different material from the production part. That difference has to be written down. If the drawing is silent, the factory will build the study.

The study may use one material. The drawing names both.

What a print can teach

Printed polymer is a fast way to hold a shape. He uses it to learn a clip, a hinge gap, a finger clearance, the way a housing splits. The object in the hand answers questions about geometry and assembly. You can see where a thumb lands. You can see whether a hook clears a rib before it seats.

The same object is silent about creep, heat, and a thread that must hold. Those are different questions. They need a coupon, or a part, in the material the factory will actually run. He writes the limit on the sheet, next to the feature, so a later reader does not treat a plastic study as proof of a metal life.

Shape is one question. Survival is another.

When the study should already be metal

Metal is the right first object when the doubt is stiffness, a wear face, or a thread. It costs more time. He spends that time on the feature that is actually in doubt, and he keeps the rest of the part plain enough to machine and plain enough to read. A complicated part that hides the question has spent the metal on the wrong thing.

The callout names a process, not a mood. A metal and its condition. A polymer and the way it will be formed. The process tells a machinist which machine, which cutter family, which care with heat. A vague compliment does not.

Plain parts keep the answer visible.

Circuits have materials too

Copper weight. The substrate. The connector that will exist in production. The wire that will not. A prototype built on whatever board was in the drawer can still answer a question about the circuit, if he records which choices were temporary. A stand-in connector gets labeled as a stand-in. The board house, the shop that fabricates the bare board, then sees the part it should load.

He chooses on purpose and writes the reason beside the callout. A manufacturer can follow a reason. A manufacturer cannot follow a preference that never made it onto the sheet.

Temporary parts get a name, or they become permanent by accident.
